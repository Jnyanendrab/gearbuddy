<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to choose: 5 criteria</h2>

<h3>1. Wall charger vs. power bank</h3>
<p>A wall charger needs an outlet but is smaller and lighter. A power bank works anywhere but weighs more because it carries its own battery cells. If you mostly work at desks and hotels with accessible outlets, a wall charger is the practical pick. If you regularly work in places without reliable power — airports, trains, outdoor locations — a power bank is worth the extra weight.</p>

<h3>2. Wattage</h3>
<p>Check the wattage printed on your laptop's OEM charger and match or exceed it. Some common reference points from manufacturer specs:</p>
<ul>
  <li>MacBook Air: 30–35W</li>
  <li>MacBook Pro 14″: 67–70W</li>
  <li>MacBook Pro 16″: 96–140W</li>
</ul>
<p>A charger rated below your laptop's recommended wattage will still work in most cases, but the laptop may charge slowly or even lose battery under heavy load.</p>

<h3>3. Port count and distribution</h3>
<p>When multiple ports are active at the same time, the total wattage is shared across them. A charger rated at 100W total might deliver only 65W to the laptop port when a phone is also plugged in. Always check the manufacturer's per-port power split chart — it's usually printed on the bottom of the charger or listed on the product page.</p>

<h3>4. Size and weight</h3>
<p>GaN chargers are roughly 40–60% smaller than equivalent silicon chargers at the same wattage, based on manufacturer size comparisons. That size advantage matters most for travel. At the desk, a compact form factor means fewer blocked outlets on a power strip.</p>

<h3>5. Airline rules</h3>
<p>The FAA limits carry-on power banks to 100 Wh (watt-hours). You can convert mAh to Wh with this formula: (mAh × voltage) ÷ 1,000 = Wh. At the standard 3.7V cell voltage, 27,000 mAh ≈ 99.9 Wh — right at the limit. The Anker 737 at 24,000 mAh comes to approximately 88.8 Wh, well within the FAA cap. Power banks above 100 Wh are generally not permitted in carry-on or checked luggage without airline approval.</p>

<h2 id="charging-safety">Charging safety</h2>

<p>USB Power Delivery (USB-PD) includes built-in negotiation between the charger and the device. Before any power flows, the charger and device agree on a voltage and current that both can handle safely. This handshake prevents a charger from pushing more power than a device is designed to accept. That said, the safety of the handshake depends on the quality of the charger's power delivery controller — which is why buying from established brands matters.</p>

<p>Look for chargers that carry recognized safety certifications: UL (Underwriters Laboratories) in the US, CE marking in Europe, and FCC compliance for electromagnetic interference. These certifications indicate that the product has been evaluated against standardized safety criteria. Avoid no-name chargers that lack certification markings entirely, regardless of their advertised wattage or GaN claims.</p>

<p>For more information on USB charging standards, visit the <a href="https://www.usb.org/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">USB Implementers Forum (USB-IF)</a>. For lithium battery safety guidance, see the <a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Batteries" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) battery safety page</a>. For airline rules on portable chargers, check the <a href="https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/more-info/?hazmat=7" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">FAA PackSafe page on portable chargers and batteries</a>.</p>

<p><em>This is general information, not safety guidance for your specific setup. Follow your device manufacturer's charging recommendations.</em></p>

  <h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>

  <div class="faq-list">
    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>Can a power bank charge a laptop?</summary>
      <p>Yes, as long as the power bank supports USB Power Delivery at a wattage your laptop can use. Most modern USB-C laptops — including MacBook Air, MacBook Pro, Dell XPS, Lenovo ThinkPad, and HP Spectre models — accept USB-C PD charging. The key is matching wattage: a 20W power bank can technically charge a laptop, but it will be extremely slow and may not keep up with the laptop's power draw under load. For comfortable laptop charging, look for a power bank that outputs at least 45W via USB-C, and ideally 65W or higher. The Anker 737 outputs up to 140W, which covers even high-wattage laptops like the MacBook Pro 16″.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>Is it safe to leave a GaN charger plugged in all the time?</summary>
      <p>Generally, yes. GaN chargers from reputable brands include overcurrent, overvoltage, and overtemperature protections. When no device is connected, a well-designed charger draws negligible standby power and does not generate meaningful heat. That said, it's good practice to unplug any charger during extended absences (vacations, etc.) as a general electrical safety precaution. This applies equally to GaN and silicon chargers.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>Can I take the Anker 737 on a plane?</summary>
      <p>Based on manufacturer specifications, the Anker 737 has a capacity of 24,000 mAh at 3.7V, which equals approximately 88.8 Wh. The FAA allows lithium-ion power banks up to 100 Wh in carry-on luggage without airline approval, so the 737 falls well within that limit. Power banks are generally not permitted in checked luggage regardless of capacity. Always confirm your specific airline's policy before traveling, as rules can vary by carrier and destination.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>What's the difference between a GaN charger and a regular charger?</summary>
      <p>The core difference is the semiconductor material. Traditional chargers use silicon transistors to convert AC wall power to DC device power. GaN chargers use Gallium Nitride transistors instead. GaN switches electrical current more efficiently, generates less heat, and allows the charger's internal circuitry to be packed into a smaller enclosure. The practical result is a charger that's roughly 40–60% smaller than a silicon charger at the same wattage, runs cooler, and wastes less energy during the conversion process. From the device's perspective, there's no difference — both deliver the same USB-C Power Delivery output.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>How many watts do I need to fast-charge an iPhone?</summary>
      <p>iPhones support fast charging via USB-C Power Delivery starting at 20W. Apple's own 20W and 30W USB-C adapters are popular choices. Beyond 30W, returns diminish — the iPhone's internal charge controller limits incoming power to protect battery health, so a 100W charger won't charge an iPhone any faster than a 30W charger. For general future-proofing, any charger or power bank capable of 20–30W USB-C PD output covers current and near-future iPhone models.</p>
    </details>
  </div>

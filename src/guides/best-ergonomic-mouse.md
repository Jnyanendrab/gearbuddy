<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to choose: 5 criteria</h2>

<p><strong>1. Hand size.</strong> Measure from the crease at your wrist to the tip of your middle finger. Under 17 cm generally fits a small mouse, 17–19 cm fits a medium, and over 19 cm fits a large. Getting the size right matters more than brand — a mouse that's too small forces your hand into a claw, and one that's too big makes you reach awkwardly for buttons.</p>

<p><strong>2. Grip style.</strong> Palm grip users (the whole hand rests on the mouse body) tend to adapt well to vertical mice. Fingertip and claw grip users — where only the tips of the fingers contact the mouse — often find vertical designs uncomfortable because the angled body doesn't support those grip styles. If you're a fingertip gripper, a sculpted horizontal mouse may suit you better.</p>

<p><strong>3. Vertical vs. sculpted horizontal.</strong> Vertical mice (57° or steeper) maximize pronation correction by rotating your forearm to the handshake position. Sculpted horizontal mice keep the hand closer to a traditional orientation but use contouring — arched sides, thumb shelves, raised palms — to partially correct wrist angle. Vertical is the bigger ergonomic shift; sculpted horizontal is the easier transition.</p>

<p><strong>4. Connectivity.</strong> Bluetooth is best for multi-device setups — pair with your laptop, tablet, and desktop and switch between them. A 2.4 GHz dongle (like Logi Bolt) offers lower latency and a more stable connection, which is useful for responsive cursor tracking. Wired USB-C eliminates all wireless variables but ties you to a cable.</p>

<p><strong>5. Weight.</strong> Lighter mice (under 100 g) reduce cumulative strain across thousands of daily micro-movements. Heavier mice (120–145 g) can actually feel more stable under a relaxed palm grip because your hand doesn't have to squeeze to control them. Neither approach is universally better — it depends on how you hold the mouse and how long you use it each day.</p>

<h2 id="ergonomics">Why wrist position matters</h2>

<p>When you use a standard flat mouse, your forearm rotates inward so your palm faces the desk — a position called pronation. In this posture, the two forearm bones (the radius and ulna) cross over each other, compressing muscles, tendons, and nerves. Over hours of daily use, that compression can contribute to discomfort, fatigue, and repetitive strain injuries.</p>

<p>A vertical or semi-vertical mouse corrects this by tilting the grip surface so your hand rests in the "handshake position" — thumb on top, pinky on the bottom, forearm bones running parallel. This reduces the muscular effort needed to hold and move the mouse. It also reduces ulnar deviation, the sideways bending of the wrist that happens when a flat mouse is positioned to the right of a keyboard.</p>

<p>Mouse ergonomics is one piece of a larger workstation setup. Monitor height, chair support, keyboard position, and break frequency all matter. For comprehensive workstation guidance, these resources are helpful starting points:</p>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>

<div class="faq-list">
  <details class="faq-item">
    <summary>Do vertical mice actually help with carpal tunnel?</summary>
    <p>Vertical mice reduce forearm pronation — the inward rotation that compresses the carpal tunnel area. By placing your hand in the handshake position (thumb on top), the forearm bones run parallel rather than crossing, which reduces pressure on the median nerve. Many users report reduced wrist discomfort after switching. However, carpal tunnel syndrome has multiple contributing factors (wrist flexion, repetitive motion, genetics, other health conditions), and a mouse alone may not resolve it. A vertical mouse is a reasonable low-risk step, but it's not a substitute for medical evaluation if you have persistent symptoms.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="faq-item">
    <summary>How long does it take to get used to a vertical mouse?</summary>
    <p>Most people report an adjustment period of about one to two weeks. The first few days tend to feel noticeably awkward — cursor control is less precise, and your hand may tire from using unfamiliar muscle groups. By the end of the first week, basic tasks usually feel natural. Full comfort with fine-detail work (design tools, precise clicking) commonly takes closer to two weeks. Keeping your old mouse nearby during the transition and switching back for short stretches can make the adjustment less frustrating.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="faq-item">
    <summary>Can I use the Logitech Lift for gaming?</summary>
    <p>The Lift works fine for casual and single-player games — strategy, turn-based RPGs, point-and-click titles, and similar genres where split-second aim isn't critical. Its 4,000 DPI sensor and standard polling rate (125 Hz) handle everyday use well but fall short of what competitive gaming demands. Dedicated gaming mice routinely offer 8,000–26,000 DPI and 1,000–8,000 Hz polling rates. The 57° vertical grip can also feel unfamiliar for rapid flick movements. If you need an ergonomic mouse specifically for competitive gaming, the Lift is not the right choice.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="faq-item">
    <summary>What's the difference between Bluetooth and Logi Bolt?</summary>
    <p>Both are wireless, but they work differently. Bluetooth connects directly to your computer's built-in Bluetooth radio — no dongle needed, easy to pair with laptops and tablets. Logi Bolt is Logitech's proprietary 2.4 GHz USB receiver, which plugs into a USB-A port. Logi Bolt generally offers lower latency and a more reliable connection, especially in environments with a lot of wireless interference (offices with many Bluetooth devices nearby). Bluetooth is more convenient for travel and multi-device use. Both the Lift and MX Master 3S support both options, so you can use whichever fits your setup.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="faq-item">
    <summary>Is the MX Master 3S worth the upgrade over the MX Master 3?</summary>
    <p>The two models share the same body shape and general feature set. The 3S adds three changes: an upgraded 8,000 DPI sensor (up from 4,000 DPI on the MX Master 3), Quiet Click switches rated at approximately 90% less click noise, and Logi Bolt receiver compatibility (the MX Master 3 used the older Unifying receiver). If you already own an MX Master 3 and it's working well, the upgrade is modest. If you're buying fresh and choosing between the two, the 3S is the better starting point for the quieter switches and current-generation receiver support.</p>
  </details>

GaN — Gallium Nitride — is a semiconductor material that has largely replaced traditional silicon in modern charger designs. Compared to silicon, GaN switches electrical current far more efficiently, generates significantly less heat in the process, and allows charger circuitry to be miniaturized into a fraction of the original footprint — often 50–70% smaller at equivalent wattage. For desk setups, that means fewer bulky adapters cluttering your power strip, less heat radiating off your charging hardware, and a cleaner, more streamlined look overall. For travel, GaN chargers are a revelation: a single compact brick can replace three or four separate adapters, stays cool enough to tuck into a bag right after use, and typically falls well within TSA carry-on regulations. All information in this guide is based on manufacturer specs and public user reviews.

What to look for in a power bank

Not all power banks are created equal, and choosing the wrong one for your use case can mean running out of charge at the worst possible moment — or carrying far more weight than you need. Here are the most important specifications to understand before buying:

  • Capacity (mAh): Milliamp-hours measure how much total energy the bank stores. As a rough guide, 10,000 mAh will give most smartphones two to three full charges; 20,000 mAh gets you five or more phone charges or one to two partial laptop charges; 24,000 mAh and above enters serious laptop-charging territory and can sustain a full day of work away from an outlet. Keep in mind that real-world capacity is typically 85–90% of the rated figure due to conversion losses.
  • Max output wattage: Wattage determines how fast a connected device charges. For phone fast charging, look for at least 20W USB-C Power Delivery. For ultrabooks and thin-and-light laptops, 45W is the practical minimum; 65W is more comfortable. MacBook Pro users should look for 96W or higher output on the top port. If you have a power-hungry gaming laptop, check whether it accepts USB-C charging at all and what wattage it needs — many require 100W+.
  • Number and type of ports: A single high-wattage USB-C port is fine for one laptop, but most users benefit from at least two USB-C PD ports plus a USB-A port for older accessories. When multiple ports are active simultaneously, the power is split — check how the power bank distributes wattage across ports under load, since some designs heavily deprioritize the USB-A port.
  • Passthrough charging: This feature lets you plug the power bank into a wall outlet and charge both the bank itself and a connected device at the same time — useful at a desk where you want to top off the bank overnight while still using it. Not all power banks support passthrough, and those that do may limit the charging rate on the connected device while in passthrough mode.
  • Display type: Lower-end power banks use a row of four LEDs to indicate remaining charge in rough 25% increments, which is imprecise when you need to know if you have enough juice for a three-hour flight. Higher-end models like the Anker 737 include a numeric digital display showing the exact percentage remaining and the current wattage flowing in or out — far more useful for planning purposes.
  • Airline restrictions: The FAA and most international aviation authorities cap carry-on power banks at 100 Wh (watt-hours). To convert mAh to Wh: multiply mAh by the battery voltage (typically 3.7V) and divide by 1,000. At 3.7V, 26,800 mAh ≈ 99.2 Wh — just under the limit. Banks rated above 100 Wh (or over approximately 26,800 mAh at 3.7V) are generally not permitted in carry-on or checked luggage on commercial flights. Always confirm with your airline before traveling.

Frequently asked questions

Is GaN technology safe?

Yes — GaN chargers are designed and certified to meet the same safety standards as traditional chargers, including UL, CE, FCC, and other regional certifications depending on the market. Because GaN operates more efficiently and generates less heat than silicon, it actually reduces thermal stress on internal components, which is a primary cause of charger degradation over time. That said, as with any electrical product, it is important to buy from reputable brands that clearly display their safety certifications and do not cut corners on components. Avoid uncertified no-name chargers regardless of the semiconductor technology they claim to use.

What wattage do I need to charge a laptop?

The required wattage varies significantly by laptop model. Most thin-and-light ultrabooks (such as the Dell XPS 13 or MacBook Air) charge comfortably at 45–65W. Apple's MacBook Pro 14" recommends 67W for standard use, though it can charge (more slowly) at lower wattages. The MacBook Pro 16" recommends 96W for full-speed charging and may charge slowly or even discharge slightly under load with anything less. Gaming laptops are more demanding still — many require 130W or higher, and some do not support USB-C charging at all, relying instead on proprietary barrel connectors. The best approach is to check the wattage printed on your existing power adapter and match or exceed that figure when choosing a GaN charger or power bank.

Can the Anker 737 charge a MacBook Pro?

Based on manufacturer specs and public user reviews, yes — the Anker 737 Power Bank outputs up to 140W via its primary USB-C port, which exceeds Apple's recommended 96W for the MacBook Pro 14" and 16". It is widely reported by users to charge MacBook Pro models at full speed when connected via a compatible USB-C cable. For the most accurate compatibility information for your specific MacBook Pro configuration, consult Anker's official product page and Apple's USB-C charging documentation.

What's the difference between a GaN charger and a power bank?

A GaN charger is a wall adapter — it converts AC power from an outlet into DC power for your devices, but it has no internal battery and cannot supply power on its own away from a wall socket. A power bank (also called a portable charger) stores electrical energy in its own internal battery cells and can charge your devices anywhere, with no outlet required. Some products combine both functions in a single unit: the Anker 737, for example, can plug directly into a wall outlet to charge connected devices like a traditional adapter, and it also carries its own 24,000 mAh internal battery to work as a standalone power bank. The Ugreen Nexode 100W, by contrast, is a wall charger only — no internal battery, no portable charging capability.

How many watts do I need for phone charging?

Standard USB charging delivers 5W, which is slow by modern standards. Fast charging for most Android smartphones operates at 18–45W depending on the phone's supported protocol (USB-C PD, Qualcomm Quick Charge, or a proprietary standard from brands like OnePlus or Samsung). iPhones currently fast-charge most effectively at 20–30W via USB-C Power Delivery — Apple's own 30W adapter is a popular choice. Beyond 30W for smartphones, the returns diminish quickly: the phone's internal charge controller will limit the incoming wattage to protect the battery, so a 100W charger does not charge a phone any faster than a 30–45W charger once you are past the phone's rated ceiling. For general future-proofing, a charger capable of 20–30W USB-C PD output covers virtually all current and near-future smartphones.
